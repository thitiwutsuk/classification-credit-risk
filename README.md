## Business Questions

From a banking risk-management perspective, this project answers:

- **Q1** — What factors contribute to a customer being Credit Risky?
- **Q2** — What behavior distinguishes a Credit Worthy customer?

## Dataset

Two source files live in [`data/`](data/) (full column-level documentation in [`data/dataset_description.md`](data/dataset_description.md)):

| File | Rows | Description |
|---|---|---|
| `data/customer_data.csv` | 1,125 customers | Demographic and encoded categorical features, plus the target `label` (`1` = high risk, `0` = low risk / worthy) |
| `data/payment_data.csv` | 8,250 records | Card payment history — overdue counts/days, normal payment counts, product limits, and balances. A customer can hold multiple products, so this table has multiple rows per customer |

## Methodology (Step by Step)

[`analysis.py`](analysis.py) follows a standard applied data science workflow. Each step exists to answer a specific question about the data before any conclusion is drawn.

### 1. Load & Aggregate
`payment_data.csv` has multiple rows per customer (one per product / reporting period), while the target label lives in `customer_data.csv` at one row per customer. The payment history is aggregated to one row per `id` — overdue counts and normal-payment counts are **summed** (total activity), balances and limits are summarized with **mean and max** (typical level vs. worst/highest exposure), and product codes are counted with **nunique**.

### 2. Clean the Data
After merging, missing values are handled based on what "missing" means for that column: count-like fields (e.g. overdue counts) are filled with `0` — no record means the event never happened — while monetary fields (limits, balances) are filled with the **median**, which is robust to the outlier balances a few customers carry.

### 3. Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA)
Every feature's mean is compared between the Risky (`label=1`) and Worthy (`label=0`) groups, expressed as a **percentage difference** so gaps are comparable across features on very different scales (some are in the hundreds of thousands, others are single-digit counts). Correlation with the label and categorical distribution (`crosstab`) are checked in parallel to triangulate the same signal from multiple angles.

### 4. Feature Engineering
An `overdue_ratio` feature is derived (`overdue count / (overdue count + normal payment count)`). Raw overdue counts are misleading on their own — 5 overdue payments out of 100 is a very different customer than 5 out of 6 — so this ratio captures the *behavior*, not just the raw tally.

### 5. Preprocessing
Categorical codes (`fea_1, fea_3, fea_5, fea_6, fea_7, fea_9`) are one-hot encoded, since they carry no ordinal meaning. The data is split 75/25 into train/test with **stratification** to preserve the 80/20 class ratio in both sets. Features are standardized (`StandardScaler`) before Logistic Regression so coefficients are comparable across features of different scales. Both models use `class_weight="balanced"` to counteract the class imbalance — otherwise a model can reach 80% accuracy by always predicting "Worthy."

### 6. Modeling
Two models are trained so their conclusions can be cross-checked against each other:

| | Logistic Regression | Random Forest |
|---|---|---|
| Relationship captured | Linear | Non-linear, with interactions |
| Interpretability | High — coefficient sign & magnitude | Moderate — feature importance |
| Gives direction (risk ↑/↓)? | Yes | No (importance only) |
| Needs feature scaling? | Yes | No |

### 7. Evaluation
Because the classes are imbalanced, accuracy alone is a poor guide — always predicting "Worthy" already scores 80%. **ROC-AUC** is used as the primary metric (discriminative power independent of a decision threshold), alongside a confusion matrix and per-class precision/recall, since in a credit-risk setting missing a genuinely risky customer (false negative) typically costs more than being overly cautious about a good one (false positive).

### 8. Interpretation
Feature rankings are triangulated from three independent signals — logistic regression coefficients, random forest importances, and the raw EDA percentage gaps — and only where all three agree is a factor reported as a driver of risk. This is then translated into the plain-language answers to Q1 and Q2 below.

> For a deeper, more technical walkthrough of every technique (with the underlying math for the sigmoid function, bagging, gradient descent, etc.), see [`METHODOLOGY.md`](METHODOLOGY.md).

## Results

### Q1 — What makes a customer Credit Risky?

Risk is driven primarily by **payment behavior**, not demographics:

- **Higher outstanding balances** — risky customers' `highest_balance` runs more than 2x higher on average than worthy customers'
- **More frequent overdue payments** — both `overdue_ratio` and total overdue days are markedly higher
- **Fewer on-time payments** — `pay_normal_sum` is roughly 30% lower than the worthy group
- Encoded demographic features (`fea_1, fea_3, fea_5, fea_6, fea_7, fea_9`) show comparatively **minor** differences between groups

**In short**: high debt + frequent overdue payments + inconsistent on-time payments define the core risky profile.

### Q2 — How does a Credit Worthy customer behave?

The mirror image of the risky profile:

- Keeps outstanding balances **low relative to available credit**
- Is **rarely overdue** — average overdue ratio around 3%
- Pays **consistently and on time**, with a clearly higher count of normal payments

**In short**: consistent on-time payment behavior and controlled debt, not demographic background, are what mark a customer as credit worthy.

## Limitations

- Model discriminative power (ROC-AUC ≈ 0.68) indicates real but moderate predictive signal — expected given the dataset's small size (1,125 customers) and class imbalance (only 20% risky)
- These are **correlational**, not **causal**, findings — e.g. a high balance can be both a symptom and a cause of risk. Treat results as a decision-support signal, not a standalone verdict
